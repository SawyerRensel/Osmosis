## Points of sail

A passage plan starts with the chart, the tide tables and the forecast, read together rather than one at a time. The tide sets the window, the wind decides whether the window is worth using, and the chart says where the hazards sit when the water is low.

Most of a day sail is spent doing very little: watching the luff of the jib, nudging the tiller, checking the sky to windward. The skill is noticing small changes early, so that a reef goes in while it is still easy rather than when it has become urgent.

Lines on a small boat are colour-coded where possible, so a new crew member can be told to ease the red one without having to learn every name on the first afternoon. The names come later, one at a time, as each line gets used.

Anchoring well is mostly about choosing the spot: enough swinging room, good holding, shelter from where the wind will be overnight rather than where it is now. Letting out enough scope matters more than the size of the anchor.

- Sailing with the wind directly behind the boat is running. ^os-stk001

- Sailing at right angles to the wind is a beam reach. ^os-stk002
